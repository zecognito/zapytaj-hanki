Moving to Poland: The Complete Relocation Checklist (2026)

Planning a move to Poland? Here's what to arrange before you leave, what to do when you arrive, and which paperwork you really shouldn't ignore.

Published October 25, 2026 · Last reviewed October 25, 2026

Moving to Poland can be exciting: historic cities, public transport in major urban areas, four distinct seasons and easy access to much of Europe. But before imagining weekends in Kraków and fresh pierogi, take care of the practical side: immigration, housing, healthcare and banking.

Whether you're moving from another EU country, the United States, India, Brazil or elsewhere, this checklist will help you prepare.

Important: Immigration and employment rules depend on citizenship, purpose of stay and individual circumstances. This is general information, not legal advice. Verify your situation with Polish authorities.

Check your right to live in Poland

Start with citizenship and the purpose of your stay. EU citizens generally enjoy free movement and access to employment, but residence beyond three months normally requires registration with the voivode; certain jobseekers have different rules. Registering EU residence is not the same as registering your address.

Non-EU citizens may need a visa, residence permit, or another legal basis. For many visa-exempt travelers the Schengen short-stay limit is 90 days in any rolling 180-day period, but nationality-specific arrangements and exceptions matter. Visa-free tourism does not automatically authorize work or long-term residence.

Residence permits and MOS in 2026

Since April 27, 2026, applications for temporary residence, permanent residence and EU long-term resident permits covered by the new procedure are filed electronically through the MOS portal. Check current application instructions, signatures and supporting documents early; other procedures can differ.

Prepare essential documents

Gather your passport or eligible national ID, immigration papers, employment or university documents, civil-status records if relevant, qualifications, health records and prescriptions, driving licence, insurance and proof of funds. Keep secure copies. Some documents require apostille or legalization and a sworn Polish translation; confirm requirements before paying for translations.

Choose the right Polish city

Warsaw is a major employment hub; Kraków has a large university and business community; Wrocław, Poznań and Gdańsk also attract international residents. Smaller cities may suit remote workers or people with family nearby. Compare housing costs, jobs, public transport and access to healthcare—not just photographs.

Renting an apartment

Check the full monthly cost before signing: the rent advertised may exclude building charges (czynsz administracyjny), heating, electricity, water and internet. Ask about the deposit (kaucja), notice period, contract (umowa najmu) and address-registration paperwork. Najem okazjonalny is a special lease form with additional legal formalities. Verify the landlord or authorized agent before sending money.

Understand PESEL

PESEL is a personal identification number used in Polish administration. Foreigners registering an address for a stay exceeding 30 days are generally assigned one automatically. If address registration is not possible, an application may be available where a specific legal provision requires a PESEL. The assignment service is free. PESEL is not a visa, residence permit or work authorization.

Understand meldunek

Meldunek is registration of your residential address at the relevant municipal office. Deadlines and requirements vary with citizenship and circumstances. It is separate from EU residence registration and from immigration permission. Ask your landlord which documents can confirm your right to occupy the property.

Arrange healthcare

Poland's public healthcare system is financed through NFZ (Narodowy Fundusz Zdrowia). Access depends on insurance or another statutory entitlement—not simply on living in Poland. Employment, family coverage, studies and self-employment can involve different arrangements. Check whether you need transitional private insurance and whether it meets any immigration requirements. Verify prescriptions and medication import rules before travel. The emergency number is 112.

Budget and banking

Plan for travel, temporary accommodation, deposit, rent, utilities, food, transport, insurance, administrative fees and an emergency reserve. Poland uses the złoty (PLN), not the euro. Compare bank identification requirements, fees, international transfers and currency conversion. Do not assume every bank accepts the same documents.

Work authorization and contracts

EU citizens generally have broad rights to work. Non-EU nationals may require both a lawful basis of stay and permission to work unless exempt. Confirm your exact route before starting a job. An umowa o pracę is an employment contract under labour law; an umowa zlecenie is a civil-law services contract; B2B arrangements involve running a business. Taxes, social insurance, leave and legal protections can differ.

Phone and digital government services

A Polish phone number helps with banking, appointments and deliveries. Compare prepaid and subscription plans. Profil Zaufany (Trusted Profile) supports identity verification for eligible online government services; mObywatel is the official digital-services app. Available features vary by eligibility and status.

Transportation and driving

Major cities offer buses and trams; Warsaw also has a metro. Intercity rail is often practical. If driving, check whether your licence is recognized, whether an International Driving Permit is needed and when exchange requirements apply. The answer depends on the issuing country and your residence circumstances.

Everyday Polish

Learning a few phrases makes errands easier: Dzień dobry (hello), Dziękuję (thank you), Proszę (please/you're welcome), Przepraszam (excuse me), Nie mówię po polsku (I don't speak Polish), and Potrzebuję pomocy (I need help).

Moving timeline checklist

Two to three months before

  • Confirm immigration and work eligibility.
  • Check documents, translations and insurance.
  • Research cities and make a realistic budget.

One month before

  • Arrange travel and initial accommodation.
  • Confirm employer or university paperwork.
  • Check medication rules and secure copies.

First week

  • Confirm accommodation and phone access.
  • Check address-registration duties and healthcare access.
  • Find your municipal office and transport options.

First month

  • Complete registrations by the applicable legal deadlines—do not assume one month is always allowed.
  • Obtain PESEL if eligible and needed.
  • Confirm banking, insurance, tax and immigration obligations.

Common mistakes

Do not confuse visa-free entry with long-term residence; PESEL with a residence permit; or the advertised rent with total housing costs. Do not wait until your legal stay expires to prepare applications, and never assume rules are identical for all nationalities.

Frequently asked questions

Can I move without speaking Polish?

Yes, if you meet the applicable immigration requirements. English may help in larger cities, but Polish is valuable for administration and daily life.

Does every foreigner need to apply for PESEL?

No. Some receive it automatically through qualifying address registration; others may need a separate application based on a legal requirement.

Can I work on a tourist visa?

Do not assume so. Work rights depend on citizenship, status and work-authorization rules.

Is Poland in the EU and Schengen?

Yes. Poland is in both and uses the złoty.

Can I apply for residence online?

The principal residence-permit procedures covered by MOS moved to electronic submission on April 27, 2026. Check official instructions for your application category.

Final thoughts

Start with your right to stay, organize your documents, understand the full cost of housing and arrange appropriate healthcare. Then tackle the smaller tasks one at a time. Welcome to Poland.
